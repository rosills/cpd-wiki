# Wei & Marco (2025) — the fermented cabbage metabolome and intestinal barrier protection

Wei L, Marco ML. *The fermented cabbage metabolome and its protection against cytokine-induced intestinal barrier disruption of Caco-2 monolayers.* Applied and Environmental Microbiology 2025;91(5):e02234-24. Open access. UC Davis; funded by a California Department of Food and Agriculture specialty-crop grant. Read 27 October 2026.

A laboratory study asking whether the **soluble metabolites** of sauerkraut — not the live bacteria — can protect gut epithelium from inflammatory damage. Bacteria-free, pH-neutralised filtrates of fermented cabbage protected Caco-2 monolayers against an IFN-γ + TNF-α challenge; raw cabbage, brine and salt did not. Three fermentation-enriched compounds (lactate, D-phenyl-lactate, indole-3-lactate) tested alone or together gave only partial protection, so the authors argue the benefit comes from the whole metabolite mixture. Relevant to [fermented foods and the gut barrier](../topics/fermented-foods-and-gut-barrier.md).

## Design

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Model | Differentiated, polarised Caco-2 monolayers (Transwell), challenged basolaterally with IFN-γ then TNF-α |
| Test material | Cell-free, filtered, pH 7.4-adjusted homogenates (~10 g cabbage + brine), applied apically at 10% v/v |
| Comparators | Raw cabbage + brine (day 0); raw cabbage in water; matched NaCl solution; untreated and cytokine-only controls |
| Ferments | Lab ferments (2% salt, 22 °C) with or without added *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* NCIMB8826R, sampled day 7 and 14; commercial sauerkraut (6 jars, one US brand) |
| Outcomes | Trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TER); paracellular permeability to 4 kDa FITC-dextran; IL-8 secretion; *TNFR2*, *MLCK*, *CLDN2* expression |
| Metabolomics | Untargeted GC-TOF/MS and RP-LC-HRMS/MS; targeted quantification of GABA, phenyl-lactate and indole-3-lactate |

## Findings

- **Barrier protection:** all fermented preparations (lab, lab + *L. plantarum*, commercial) kept TER and FITC-dextran permeability at untreated-control levels despite the cytokine challenge. Raw cabbage gave no protection; salt alone gave an intermediate, non-significant effect on permeability.
- **Inflammation dissociated from barrier:** IL-8 stayed high with the lab ferments and fell only modestly with the commercial product (and, unexpectedly, with raw cabbage). Barrier-related gene expression was largely unchanged. The protection therefore seems to act downstream of, or separately from, the inflammatory signal — the authors speculate about PPAR-γ (phenyl-lactate) and AhR (indole-3-lactate) pathways.
- **Metabolome:** fermentation clearly separated ferments from raw cabbage. Sugars (sucrose, raffinose, glucose, fructose) fell; lactate, mannitol, sorbitol, amino acids and derivatives, many small peptides, phenolics and some vitamins rose. Over 100 compounds were consistently increased across all ferments.
- **Named bioactives:** GABA roughly doubled to ~400 µg/mL; D-phenyl-lactate rose from ~6 to ~20 µg/mL; indole-3-lactate rose modestly (~8.5 to ~10 µg/mL) and only in the commercial and *L. plantarum* ferments.
- **Starter culture:** adding *L. plantarum* made the lab ferment dominate quickly, acidify further and resemble the commercial product metabolically.
- **Single compounds:** lactate, phenyl-lactate and indole-3-lactate (alone or mixed, at ferment-like concentrations) normalised FITC-dextran permeability but did not sustain TER and did not reduce IL-8.
- **Biogenic amines:** the commercial product had higher **tyramine** and **putrescine**, possibly from refrigerated storage.
- **Live bacteria varied widely in shop-bought jars:** culturable lactic acid bacteria ranged from ~10² to ~10⁷ CFU/mL between jars.

## Appraisal (agent synthesis)

- **Strength:** good mechanistic design — bacteria removed, pH and salt controlled, raw-cabbage comparator, two complementary barrier measures, broad metabolomics.
- **In vitro only.** Caco-2 is a transformed colon-cancer line; no mucus layer, immune cells, enteric nerves or microbiota. Filtrates bypass digestion and absorption. No human outcome is measured.
- **Dose:** apical exposure equates to a diluted ~10 g serving, which is reassuring for plausibility, but delivery to the epithelium in vivo is unknown.
- **Small and narrow:** six replicates per group; one commercial brand; one cabbage type; two lab fermentation runs.
- **Discordant end-points** (TER vs FITC-dextran vs IL-8) show that "barrier protection" depends on how it's measured.
- **Context from the paper's own references:** human evidence for sauerkraut is limited — a small pilot RCT in IBS reported symptom improvement with both pasteurised and unpasteurised sauerkraut (Nielsen et al., *Food Funct* 2018), which fits the idea that metabolites, not just live organisms, matter; a crossover trial on sauerkraut and gut microbiota was published in 2025 (Schropp et al., *Microbiome*). Neither has been read for this wiki.

> **Open question:** Do these findings replicate in organoid or non-transformed epithelial models, and in human studies measuring barrier function (e.g. lactulose–mannitol or zonulin, with their known limitations)? Look at the Nielsen 2018 and Schropp 2025 trials.
